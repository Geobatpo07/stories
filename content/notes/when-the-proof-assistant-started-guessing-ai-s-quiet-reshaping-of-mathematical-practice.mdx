## The quiet shift

Mathematics has always resisted automation at its core: a proof is not a
computation, it is an argument, and arguments were supposed to need a mind
willing to be convinced. That assumption is now being tested from two
directions at once — large language models that produce plausible proof
sketches on demand, and formal proof assistants (Lean, Coq, Isabelle) that
can check, and increasingly suggest, the steps a human would have had to
find alone.

## What actually changed

- Automated systems (AlphaProof, AlphaGeometry, and the growing ecosystem
  around Lean's Mathlib) now solve competition-level problems that were,
  a few years ago, evidence of genuine mathematical talent.
- The bottleneck in formalizing a result has shifted from "can a machine
  check this" to "can a human state this precisely enough for a machine
  to check" — arguably a harder and more interesting problem.
- Mathematicians increasingly treat these tools the way physicists treat
  simulation: not a source of truth, but a fast way to rule out wrong paths.

## What this note is not claiming

This is not a story about machines "understanding" mathematics, nor about
proof becoming obsolete. It is a note on practice: on what a working
mathematician's day looks like differently now, and what remains stubbornly,
interestingly human about the parts AI has not touched.

## Open questions

- Does formal verification change *which* conjectures get attempted?
- Does it narrow mathematical taste toward what is easy to formalize?
